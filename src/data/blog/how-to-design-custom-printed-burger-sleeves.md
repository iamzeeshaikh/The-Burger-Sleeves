# How to Design Custom Burger Sleeves for Printing

A blank sleeve is a small billboard waiting for a design decision. It will circle a hot burger, ride in a hand, and appear in photographs, all within arm's length of a paying customer. Custom burger sleeves earn all that attention only when the artwork survives folding, grease, and the printing process itself. Design for sleeves means design for a curved, creased, food-adjacent surface. The design rules differ from posters and screens in specific, entirely learnable ways. This guide walks the design process from dieline to production order.

## Start with the sleeve dieline

Good sleeve design begins with a template, not with inspiration. The template is free and the rework it prevents is not.

Every sleeve begins as a flat die-cut blank, and the dieline is its map. The drawing shows the cut edges, the fold or glue zones, and the printable area between them.

Request the dieline before any design work starts. The [sleeve formats across The Burger Sleeves range](/) each publish their template on request. Art built on a guessed rectangle needs rework once the real template arrives. Each sleeve size carries its own dieline, so a two-size program needs two separately prepared files. The glue lap deserves special attention, since ink in the glue zone weakens the bond. Suppliers mark these zones clearly on the template, and designers who respect them save an entire proof round. Ten minutes with the template prevents the classic first-order mistakes.

## Identify the visible print areas

Not every square inch of the blank earns ink equally. Knowing which zones stay visible focuses the budget where eyes actually land.

A sleeve in service shows two main faces, front and back panels around the band. The glue seam hides a strip, and the customer's hand covers another zone during eating.

Map the faces before placing any element on them. The front panel faces the customer at the counter and in most photos taken. The back panel faces outward in the other hand position, doubling the impression. Panels on [cafe burger sleeve formats](/product/cafe-burger-sleeves/) run wide enough for a logo plus a short message per face. Elements that cross from one panel to another will bend around the burger's physical curve. Patterns handle that curve gracefully, while straight-edged boxes and frames warp visibly the moment the band closes.

## Position the logo deliberately

Placement decides whether the logo works for its money. The same mark succeeds or vanishes depending on where it lands.

The logo is the reason the print run exists, so its placement leads. Center it on the front panel at a size readable from across a busy counter.

A drink-coaster scale works as the practical size benchmark. Smaller marks disappear in customer photos, and oversized marks crop awkwardly at the band edges. Keep the mark well clear of the seam line, where any overlap would slice it. Repeat a smaller version on the back panel rather than enlarging one mark to wrap the whole band. Vertical placement matters just as much, since hands cover the band's lower half during eating. The upper third of the panel stays visible through the whole meal, which makes it the prime slot.

## Practical colors for custom burger sleeves

Sleeve printing runs efficiently at one or two colors, and most memorable sleeve designs use exactly that many. Restraint reads confident at this small scale.

Color selection follows the stock and the food:

- Black on kraft, the highest-contrast classic that suits every menu
- White ink on kraft or colored stock for a premium look
- Warm reds and oranges that flatter food without clashing
- Deep greens and blues for coffee-adjacent and health-leaning brands
- Two-color pairings with one dark anchor and one accent

Kraft's brown base tints everything printed over it, muting cool tones toward earth shades. Always test brand colors on the actual stock rather than trusting screen values. Event programs using [event burger sleeve runs](/product/event-burger-sleeves/) often adapt the brand palette per occasion while keeping the logo constant.

## Design around folds and food contact

Two physical realities shape every sleeve file. The board folds, and the food leaks.

The sleeve's folds cross the artwork, and grease reaches its lower edge. Both facts belong in the design file, not in the complaint email afterward.

Keep all type at least 5 mm clear of every fold and cut edge. Let backgrounds and patterns run freely across folds, since solid fields forgive creasing in ways text never does. The interior face usually stays unprinted for direct food contact simplicity. If interior print truly matters to the concept, food-safe low-migration inks become the requirement, confirmed in writing. Plan the lower band as a visual sacrifice zone, where grease may appear on absorbent stocks over a meal. Light colors hide grease the worst, which is one more argument for placing dark anchors low on the panel.

## Review proofs properly

Everything before this step was theory, and proofs are the evidence. Treat the proof round as the real deliverable it is.

Screens mislead about paper, and sleeve stocks mislead more than most. Physical proofing is short, cheap, and settles everything.

1. Approve the digital proof first for layout, spelling, and dieline fit.
2. Request a physically printed sample on your actual sleeve stock.
3. Check the color under your counter lighting, never office light.
4. Fold the proof into its final shape and inspect every crease crossing.
5. Slide it onto a real burger and photograph it exactly as customers would.
6. Sign off in writing while referencing the exact proof version number.

The burger test at step five catches everything flat review misses. Artwork that looks balanced flat can look crowded once it curves. One honest proof round is the entire quality system for a sleeve print run, and it costs a week at most.

## Plan the production order

The last mile of design is paperwork, and good paperwork pays for years. A tidy production order makes every reorder a one-line email.

Approved artwork becomes plates, and plates make reorders cheap. The first order carries the setup cost, and every following order rides on it.

State the sleeve size, stock, colors, and quantity against the approved proof number. Confirm the full production window against any launch or event date. Archive the final artwork with the job number where the next order can actually find it.
