# How to Choose Burger Sleeve Sizes

A jumbo burger wedged into a standard sleeve splits its seam by the third bite. A slider swimming in that same sleeve drops straight through it onto the tray. Both familiar failures trace back to one skipped step, which is measuring the food before ordering the paper. Burger sleeve sizes follow the food itself, and the food varies far more than most buyers assume. Bun diameter, build height, and topping load all push the final numbers around. This guide turns sleeve sizing into one short measuring session and a clear ordering decision.

## Measure the complete burger first

The kitchen holds every number the order needs. Collecting them takes a scale, a ruler, and one built burger of each kind.

Sizing starts with the assembled product, never the bun alone. Build your standard burger exactly as it serves, then measure it.

Three simple numbers per menu item cover everything the sleeve needs to know:

- Diameter at the widest point, usually the bun's equator
- Height from board to bun crown with everything stacked
- Weight of the complete finished build, since heavy burgers need deeper bands

Cooked measurements matter because buns compress and patties shrink during cooking. Raw numbers off a spec sheet mislead by whole millimeters. A menu with several builds needs each one measured separately, since similar-looking items differ more than eyes admit. Write the numbers down per item, and the rest of the process becomes simple arithmetic. The measuring session takes twenty minutes and prevents every sizing complaint that follows.

## Allow room for height and toppings

Height planning is where good measurement pays off. The band must cover enough to work while hiding as little as possible.

The sleeve band needs vertical coverage without swallowing the burger. A band that reaches the bun crown hides the food and fights every bite.

The working rule is coverage of the middle third, roughly 60 to 90 mm of band for standard builds. Toppings change the math upward immediately. Slaw, onion rings, and double cheese raise the stack and widen the equator together. Sauce-heavy builds want a slightly taller band to catch the runoff line. Leave the top third of the burger visible, since presentation is half of the format's value. The bottom edge should sit clear of the board so the sleeve never wicks moisture up from a wet tray.

## Burger sleeve sizes run on two dimensions

Every sleeve on the market reduces to a width and a depth. Reading catalogs gets easy once those two numbers make sense.

Width is the grip dimension, and it runs entirely on compression. The band's relaxed circumference should sit slightly under the burger's own girth. Those few millimeters of squeeze are the entire hold.

Depth is the band's height, and the two axes are specified independently on every order. Category pages like the [burger sleeve size range](/product-category/burger-sleeves-sizes/) list both dimensions per format, which makes the matching purely mechanical. Tolerances matter at the edges of the range. A band 5 mm too wide slides freely, and one 5 mm too narrow crushes soft brioche flat. Paper stretch gives a little forgiveness, while card stocks hold their cut size firmly. When between two sizes, take the snugger one for paper and the looser one for card.

## Slider and standard sizing

Most US menus live in two size neighborhoods, and knowing both saves over-ordering. The slider and the standard cover the daily volume.

Sliders are their own sizing world. A slider bun runs around 60 to 70 mm across, roughly half a standard burger's footprint.

Purpose-cut [slider burger sleeves](/product/slider-burger-sleeves/) hold that small scale without drowning it. Trios plated together look composed when each mini burger wears its own matching band. Standard burgers cluster around 100 to 115 mm diameters across most US menus. That natural clustering is why one standard sleeve size covers most single-patty builds without complaint. The real decision point comes at the edges of the range, where smash doubles and tall specialty builds push out of standard territory entirely.

## Plan for jumbo builds

The top of the menu deserves its own paragraph in the order. Signature builds break standard assumptions by design.

Jumbo is where sizing failures get expensive, because the burgers themselves cost the most. Half-pound doubles and loaded specialty stacks need genuine clearance in both dimensions.

Dedicated [jumbo burger sleeve formats](/product/jumbo-burger-sleeves/) run wider and taller for exactly these oversized builds. The jumbo band also works harder structurally, carrying more weight through a longer eating time. Heavier stock earns its place here even when the standard line runs on light paper. Menus with one signature giant should stock its sleeve separately rather than stretching the standard fit. The signature item is photographed the most, and its packaging fit shows up in every single shot.

## Test loading and eating

Paper sizing ends at the pass, where real food takes over. The trial below is short and tells the truth.

Numbers get you close, and a physical trial finishes the job. Run it with real cooked food during one quiet service.

1. Slide each menu item into its candidate sleeve right at the pass.
2. Lift each sleeved burger one-handed and hold it for ten seconds.
3. Eat one of each build, watching where the band sits by mid-meal.
4. Load the messiest build available and check the band for soak-through.
5. Try the fit again with service gloves on, as staff will during a rush.
6. Note any single item that fought its sleeve and resize just that one item.

The eating test reveals what static fitting always misses. Bands ride up, slide down, or pinch only when a real meal is in motion. One quiet service of testing settles a full year of ordering.

The last step converts measurements into an order that reorders itself. One good table does the work forever.

List each menu item with its measured dimensions and its assigned sleeve size. Group all the items sharing a size so the case counts consolidate cleanly.
